If you are raising a child in Istanbul in English, you probably know this moment. Your child reads English books, watches everything in English, maybe goes to an international school. Their English is not the problem any more.

The problem is what to do with it.

Istanbul has a large international community. According to the Presidency of Migration Management, as compiled by the Göç Vakfı research foundation, the city had 610,221 foreign residents with residence permits at the start of September 2026. Many of them are families. Yet when parents look for something creative for a ten- or fifteen-year-old to do in English outside school, the options thin out quickly.

This guide is about that gap: why it exists, what drama does about it, what the research says, and how to judge a programme.

## Why most options stop fitting at B1

Language levels in Europe are described with the CEFR scale, from A1 to C2. A child at B1 can describe experiences, events and plans and keep a simple conversation going. That is roughly where many children at international schools, or Turkish children with strong school English, already are.

Once a child is there, three kinds of activity are usually on offer, and none of them quite fits:

- **Language courses** keep teaching grammar and vocabulary the child mostly already has.
- **Drama and theatre courses for children** are plentiful in Istanbul, but almost all run in Turkish.
- **English drama for children** exists, but the options we found are either open to every level or built to introduce English through play. That is good design for beginners. For a child who already speaks English, it means spending the year at someone else's starting point.

What is missing is a place where English is simply the working language and the work itself is something else: making theatre.

## What B1 to B2 actually means for speaking

The Council of Europe describes spoken English at each level in some detail. Two lines are worth knowing:

- At **B1**, a speaker "can keep going comprehensibly", though pausing to plan and repair sentences is very evident.
- At **B2**, a speaker can produce stretches of language "with a fairly even tempo", even if they still hesitate at times.

In other words, a large part of the jump from B1 to B2 is not new words. It is speaking without stopping to plan every sentence. That is a skill you build by speaking under real conditions, many times, with someone listening and responding. A grammar lesson rarely provides that. A rehearsal room does, every week.

## What drama trains that a classroom does not

**Answering in the moment.** Improvisation does not allow a pause to translate. A partner says something; you answer; the scene moves on. Done weekly, this becomes normal rather than frightening.

**Repetition that does not feel like drilling.** Actors repeat lines dozens of times in rehearsal. The aim is the scene, not the sentence, but the sentence gets practised anyway, until it flows.

**Speaking to people, not to a teacher.** In a class, English is often performed for someone who will correct it. On stage it is spoken to a partner and an audience, for a reason. That changes how children use it.

**Making things.** Good youth drama is not only acting. Writing a scene, designing a character, building movement as a group: these use English as a tool for creative work, which is exactly how adults use a second language.

## What the research says (and what it does not)

There is solid research behind drama in language learning, and it is worth reading it carefully rather than generously.

- A study published in *TESOL Quarterly* (Galante & Thomson, 2017) followed 24 Brazilian teenagers learning English. Over four months, one group studied through drama, the other through standard communicative classes. Thirty native English listeners rated their speech: the drama group came out as more fluent and easier to understand. Accent did not change.
- A related study by the same researcher, also with 24 Brazilian teenagers over four months (Galante, *RELC Journal*, 2018), measured foreign language anxiety. It fell in both groups, slightly more in the drama group.
- A meta-analysis of 47 studies (Lee, Patall, Cawthon & Steingut, *Review of Educational Research*, 2015) found positive effects of drama-based teaching on achievement, and on psychological and social outcomes. The authors also note that many study designs were weak for drawing firm causal conclusions.

What this adds up to: drama is a well-supported way to build spoken fluency and comfort. It is not a replacement for grammar teaching, and no honest programme should promise a specific jump in level.

## How to choose a programme

These are the questions we would ask, whether or not you choose us.

**Is there a level threshold?** If a programme is open to every level, ask how it handles a fluent child sitting next to a beginner. A clear threshold (for example B1) means the material can start higher.

**Are age groups separated?** A ten-year-old and a sixteen-year-old need different work. Ask what the age bands are.

**How long does it run?** Fluency is built by repetition over time. Short holiday workshops are fun; a programme across the school year is what changes how a child speaks.

**What do the children make?** Ask whether children only rehearse a finished script or also write, design and devise. The second builds creativity as well as language.

**Is there a real performance?** A show in front of an audience gives the year a goal and gives children something to be proud of.

**Who teaches, and in what language do they talk to parents?** Look for named teachers with theatre training, and for staff who can speak to you in English.

## FAQ

**What English level does my child need?**
Around B1 or above: your child can follow an everyday conversation and answer in full sentences. There is no placement test.

**My child is fluent. Will drama still be challenging?**
Yes. The challenge is improvising, writing scenes, holding a character over months and performing for an audience, not vocabulary.

**Will it improve my child's English?**
Drama-based programmes have been linked to larger gains in spoken fluency than standard communicative classes (Galante & Thomson, 2017). We do not test, so we do not promise a CEFR level.
