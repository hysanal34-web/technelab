Search for English drama in Istanbul and you will mostly find classes for small children. Songs, games, English through play. That is valuable work. It is also the wrong room for a sixteen-year-old who reads novels in English and argues with their friends in it.

Teenagers are the most overlooked group in this city's English-language theatre. This guide is about what they actually need, and what to look for.

## Why teenagers stop fitting

Two things happen around fourteen or fifteen.

First, the material stops fitting. A teenager does not want to play a talking rabbit. They want scenes about things they recognise: friendship that goes wrong, parents who do not listen, a decision with a cost. If the material is childish, they switch off, and no amount of English practice survives a bored teenager.

Second, the group stops fitting. A mixed group of eight- and seventeen-year-olds rarely works for either side. The younger ones are overwhelmed, the older ones hold back. Many teenagers are at their most self-conscious in these years, and the fastest way to make a fifteen-year-old go quiet is to put them on stage next to someone half their age.

So the first question to ask any programme is simple: how are the age groups split?

## What a good teen drama class does

It treats English as the working language, not the subject. A teenager at B1 or above does not need to be taught English in a drama room. They need to use it under real pressure: answering a scene partner in the moment, holding a character's point of view they disagree with, speaking to an audience. That pressure is where fluency is built.

It gives them authorship. Teenagers are more committed to work they helped to make. Improvisation, devising and writing short scenes from their own ideas turn them from performers of someone else's script into co-creators. In practice this is also where their English becomes most personal and most precise, because they are choosing their own words.

It ends on a real stage. A class that only ever plays to itself teaches a different skill from one that ends with an audience. Knowing that the work will be seen changes how a group rehearses: lines get repeated until they flow, entrances get practised, nervousness gets managed. For many teenagers, that performance is the moment their English stops feeling like schoolwork.

It protects them while it challenges them. Improvisation asks people to fail in public. For teenagers that is a big ask. Good teachers build trust before they build scenes, and give a character as a shield: when the character makes a mistake, it is not you.

## Questions to ask before you enrol

**Is there a minimum English level?** If a programme is open to "all levels", it will usually pitch to the lowest one. For a teenager who already speaks English, a stated threshold (around B1) is a good sign.

**How are ages grouped?** Look for a separate group for older teenagers, not a single 8–17 class.

**Who writes the material?** Ask whether the final performance is a finished play handed out to memorise, or something built with the group.

**Is there a performance, and when?** A date and an audience tell you the programme is going somewhere.

**Can parents communicate in English?** For expat and international families this matters more than it should. Ask before you visit.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is English drama useful for teenagers who are already fluent?**

Yes, because the challenge is not vocabulary. Improvising with a partner, writing a scene and performing for an audience are different skills from speaking well in class, and fluent teenagers often have the most room to play.

**Do teenagers need acting experience?**

No. A good programme builds from games and trust before it asks anyone to perform. What matters more is willingness to take part every week.

**Are teenagers mixed with young children?**

In many programmes, yes. Ours separates ages 8–12 and 13–17, because the material and the pace they need are different.

**Is there a free trial?**

We run free introduction sessions every week: Sundays at 13:00 in Pera and Saturdays at 15:00 in Kadıköy.
