You know the scene if you have a teenager in Istanbul. Their English is good on paper. They read it, they write it, they understand films without subtitles. Then someone asks them a simple question in English at a family dinner, or a visiting teacher turns to them in class, and they say "yes" and look at the table.

This is not a knowledge problem. It is a different problem, and it needs a different kind of practice.

## Knowing a language and using it live are two skills

An exam rewards accuracy. You have time, you can reread, you can correct. A conversation rewards something else: speed, tolerance for being slightly wrong, and the ability to keep going when a word will not come.

Most school English builds the first skill very well and the second almost by accident. So a student can be fluent in the sense that matters to a test and still be unpractised in the sense that matters at a dinner table.

## Why teenagers in particular

Fifteen to seventeen is a self-conscious age. A mistake made in front of peers costs more than a mistake made in front of a younger child or an adult stranger. The teenager does the maths silently: if I speak and it comes out wrong, I look foolish. If I stay quiet, nobody notices. Silence wins.

Add a second pressure, common in Istanbul's bilingual and international school circles: the people around them speak English fluently, sometimes natively. The gap feels public. A teenager who would be perfectly comfortable in a room of learners may freeze in a room of fluent speakers.

None of this requires a diagnosis. It is a normal response to a social cost that has been set too high.

## What does not fix it

More vocabulary does not fix it, because vocabulary was never the missing piece. Extra grammar practice does not fix it either, and can make it worse, since it reinforces the idea that speaking is a thing to get right before you do it.

Pushing the teenager to "just talk more" tends to backfire when the setting is still high stakes. Being asked to perform on demand in front of people is exactly the situation they are avoiding.

## What does change it

The cost of being wrong has to drop. That is a design question, not a motivation question.

Improvisation and drama work do this because the format makes error ordinary. In a scene, nobody is checking grammar; the task is to respond to the other person. When the response is half a sentence and a gesture, the scene continues anyway. The teenager learns, by repeating it, that an imperfect sentence does not end anything.

A few features matter:

**A task other than "speak English."** When attention is on the scene, the language comes along with it. The student is solving a problem with a partner, not displaying their English.

**Small groups and repetition.** Doing it with the same people week after week lowers the audience effect. The room stops being an audience and becomes a group.

**Physical engagement.** Moving, reacting and playing with status take the pressure off the mouth. Speech becomes one channel among several.

**An end point that is shared.** A group heading toward a shared performance has a reason to speak that is not personal display.

## Frequently asked questions

**My teen understands everything but will not speak. Is the English level the problem?**
Usually not. Understanding and speaking are different skills. Speaking requires tolerating imperfection in real time, and that is trained by practice in low-stakes settings, not by more study.

**Should I make my teenager speak English at home?**
Pressure at home often reproduces the high-stakes situation. Low-pressure routes work better: shared activities, films watched together, or letting them choose when to switch languages.

**Do teenagers need an advanced level for English drama work?**
No. The aim is comfort with using the language, not perfect command of it. A level at which the student can express themselves in everyday situations is the usual starting point.

**Is drama just for shy students?**
No. Confident students use it to extend range, and shy students use it to lower the cost of speaking. The format works for both because it is built around response rather than display.
