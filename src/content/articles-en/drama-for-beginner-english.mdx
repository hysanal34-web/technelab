Drama can help absolute beginners in English, but not in the way it is usually sold. The research supports three things at beginner level: understanding through movement, learning ready-made phrases in context, and lowering the pressure to speak too early. It does not support the claim that drama makes beginners fluent, and for adult beginners in particular the evidence is thin. Here is what we know, what we don't, and how we design around both.

We run English drama workshops in Istanbul. We are opening a beginners' group, so we have every reason to oversell drama. We would rather you knew the limits before you joined.

## Where it works

### 1. Understanding comes through the body

The classic method for body-based language learning at beginner level is Total Physical Response (TPR), developed by James Asher. Learners first carry out commands in the new language with their bodies, and speak only when they are ready (Asher, 1969, *The Modern Language Journal*).

In Asher's experiments, students who acted out Russian commands understood them better than students who translated them, and the difference held two weeks later. The advantage was largest for longer, newly combined sentences. One more finding matters for workshop design: pushing learners to speak during training *lowered* their listening comprehension (Asher, 1968).

Two honest limits. Asher measured listening comprehension only, not speaking. And reviews of TPR and movement-based teaching describe the research base as small: a 2023 review found only five studies with university-level learners, mostly qualitative (Hooli, Ortega-Martín & Ruiz-Montero, 2023).

### 2. Beginner speech is built on phrases, and drama repeats phrases with a reason

At beginner level, speech largely rests on formulaic sequences: phrases stored and retrieved whole rather than built word by word (Wray, 2002). Studies of school learners show beginners producing questions like "what is your name?" fluently long before they can use the grammar inside them (Myles, Hooper & Mitchell, 1998). A longitudinal study of classroom learners found that those who used such phrases early and often later reached higher accuracy in the related grammar (Hammond & Gil, 2025).

Drama is a natural engine for this kind of repetition. "Can I have the key?" said by a polite character, an angry one, a shy one, is the same phrase repeated five times, but it does not feel like a drill. That bridge from phrase research to drama is our inference; it has not been tested directly at phrase level.

A small study with intermediate learners found that teaching formulaic sequences increased speech rate and the length of unbroken runs of speech, though native listeners did not perceive a significant fluency difference (McGuire & Larson-Hall, 2017). Small sample, intermediate level: a hint, not proof.

### 3. Silence is allowed, and it is not passive

Children who seem "silent" in a new language are often busy. Saville-Troike (1988) recorded young learners quietly repeating what they heard, practising and rehearsing phrases for later public use. Rehearsal followed by performance is exactly the rhythm of a drama room.

This research is about children. For adults, we take a principle from it, not a proven stage: nobody has to speak alone in the first weeks. Listening and answering with movement counts as taking part.

### 4. For children, there is some controlled evidence

In Turkey, a controlled study with 46 third-grade pupils found that six weeks of creative drama improved speaking skills significantly more than in the control group (Öztürk Pat & Yılmaz, 2021). In Wales, drama-based vocabulary teaching helped primary pupils learning Welsh as a second language (Kalogirou, Beauchamp & Whyte, 2019). Both are small studies, but they are at the right level. ## Where it doesn't

### The fluency evidence is not from beginners

The study most often cited for drama and fluency, Galante and Thomson (2017, *TESOL Quarterly*), worked with learners at roughly B1 level over a 74-hour programme. Its results cannot be carried straight to absolute beginners in a 24-hour programme. We searched for a controlled, peer-reviewed study of drama with adult A1 learners and did not find one. That is a gap in the research, and we would rather say it than paper over it. We cover the wider research in [what science says about learning English through play and imitation](/en/articles/english-creative-drama-play-imitation-science).

### Short programmes may show nothing measurable

A Turkish study of ten drama activities with fourth-graders found no quantitative difference in motivation, anxiety or attitude (Öksüzer, 2021). Short interventions may simply be too short to measure.

### Drama can itself be scary

In a survey of 137 English teachers, drama was reported to help vocabulary, participation and confidence, but teachers also listed learners' initial reluctance to take part as a real difficulty (Alelyani & Alharbi, 2025). Stage pressure is its own kind of anxiety, and it peaks in adolescence (Dewaele & MacIntyre, 2014). This is the main reason [teenagers freeze when speaking English](/en/articles/why-teenagers-freeze-speaking-english-istanbul), and why exposure has to be gradual.

### You cannot improvise with nothing

Lynne Cameron's principle for young learners holds for adults too: you cannot learn spoken language you do not understand (Cameron, 2001). Free improvisation on day one, with no phrases in hand, slides into silence or the mother tongue. Beginner drama has to run in order: input first, then structured roles, then freer scenes. That order is a design inference from the literature, not a tested protocol.

### It does not replace grammar, reading or writing

Drama shows you structures on stage. It does not teach grammar systematically, and it does nothing for reading and writing. If you need those, and most beginners do, pair drama with a course. ## What 24 hours can and cannot do

Cambridge estimates around 90–100 guided learning hours to reach A1 from zero, and 180–200 for A2. Twenty-four hours is roughly a quarter of the way to A1. It is not a level.

What we expect after 12 weeks, 2 hours a week:

- You say the phrases we practised with fewer pauses and more ease.
- You are more willing to speak.
- You can play a short scene in English with your group.

What we do not expect: fluency, a level jump, or free conversation with strangers.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can complete beginners learn English through drama?**
Yes, if the drama is built for beginners: listening and movement first, then short phrases in structured roles, then freer scenes. Drama with no language input, where beginners are asked to improvise from day one, tends to end in silence.

**Does drama make you fluent?**
Not at beginner level, and not in 24 hours. The strongest fluency evidence comes from intermediate learners in a 74-hour programme. At A1, the realistic gain is saying practised phrases with fewer pauses and being more willing to speak.

**Is Total Physical Response the same as drama?**
No. TPR is a teacher-led technique where learners respond to commands with movement. Drama is open-ended and built on roles. TPR works well as the entry layer of a beginner drama workshop.

**Is drama for beginners only for children?**
No. In Asher's age comparisons, adults learning through movement understood faster than children in the short term. The research on adult beginners and drama specifically is thin, but there is no evidence that body-based learning is only for children.
