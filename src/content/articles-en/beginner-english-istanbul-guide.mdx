If you are an adult starting English from zero in Istanbul, the honest answer is this: it takes longer than most adverts suggest, there are good options at every budget, and the best first step depends on what you need English for. Cambridge estimates around 90–100 guided learning hours to reach A1 from zero and 180–200 to reach A2. Plan in hours, not weeks, and choose a format you will actually keep going to.

This guide is for people who live in Istanbul, or have just moved here, and whose English is at or near zero. Many of them speak Turkish, Arabic, Persian, Russian or another language at home, and English is the language they need for work, study or a move abroad. We are a theatre company, not a language school, so we will tell you where we fit and where we don't.

## First, the numbers

Cambridge English publishes guided learning hours for each level of the Common European Framework (CEFR). From zero, the cumulative figures are roughly:

- **A1:** 90–100 hours
- **A2:** 180–200 hours
- **B1:** 350–400 hours

Cambridge is careful to say these are only a guide. The real number depends on your learning background, how intensively you study, your age, and how much English you hear and use outside class.

What does A1 actually mean? The CEFR describes A1 fluency as managing "very short, isolated, mainly pre-packaged utterances, with much pausing to search for expressions." In other words, at A1 nobody is fluent. You get by with ready-made phrases. That is normal, and it is the right first goal.

So when a programme promises "fluent English in three months", divide its total hours by the numbers above. Most short programmes cover a quarter of the way to A1, or less. That does not make them useless. It means the promise is wrong, not the programme.

## Is it too late for me?

No. Research on classroom learning points the other way from the popular myth. In the Barcelona Age Factor project, learners who started later generally did better at every measurement point than those who started younger, under the same limited classroom exposure (Muñoz, 2006). In James Asher's experiments with body-based language learning, adults understood new commands faster than children in the short term (Asher & Price, 1967).

Young children have long-term advantages in immersion settings. In a classroom, over months rather than decades, adults tend to move faster. Your age is not the obstacle. Hours and consistency are.

## Your options in Istanbul

### Municipal and public courses

Free, but with conditions. Enstitü İstanbul İSMEK lists beginner English on its site, but we could not open its course pages to verify current terms, so check directly with the institution. Some district municipalities run free courses with residence or age conditions; Kadıköy's Social Life Houses, for example, offer English for residents over 65. Good if your budget is zero and your schedule is flexible.

### Language schools

Kadıköy and Beyoğlu have several schools with clear A1 tracks, a level test, and evening or weekend groups. Their strength is structure: grammar, reading and writing taught in order, with a coursebook. Ask how many hours per week, how many hours in total, how big the group is, and how much of each session you will spend speaking.

### Online platforms and private tutors

Flexible and available from home. One honest note from the platforms themselves: Cambly's support page recommends that true beginners start with more structured one-to-one plans, and Preply's FAQ estimates around 300 hours for basic conversational fluency. Free-conversation formats are hard when you have no phrases yet.

### Conversation groups and language exchanges

Social and cheap or free. Kadıköy has a long-running weekly language-exchange meetup. Most of these assume you already have some phrases, so they work better from A2 upwards. At zero, you may end up listening all evening. That is not wasted time, but it is slow.

### Drama-based workshops

This is where we sit. In a drama workshop, you learn phrases inside short scenes, with your body and with other people, and repeat them in different situations until they come out with fewer pauses. We explain the research, and its limits, in [why drama works for absolute beginners, and where it doesn't](/en/articles/drama-for-beginner-english). Short version: the evidence is promising for children and for movement-based vocabulary learning, and thin for adult beginners. We say so openly.

## A realistic plan for your first 12 weeks

1. **Pick one structured source.** A school, a course, or a tutor. Something that gives you grammar and reading in order.
2. **Add one speaking space where you are not the only beginner.** A beginners' group, not a mixed-level club.
3. **Add daily exposure.** Ten minutes of listening a day counts. Cambridge's figures assume time outside class too.
4. **Measure what is measurable.** Not "am I fluent?", but "can I introduce myself, ask for something, and say how I feel without stopping?"

Language anxiety matters here. A meta-analysis of 105 samples found a moderate negative relationship between language anxiety and achievement (Teimouri, Goetze & Plonsky, 2019). A setting where you are not judged is not a luxury at A1; it affects whether you keep going.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does it take to learn English from zero?**
Cambridge estimates around 90–100 guided learning hours to reach A1 and 180–200 to reach A2, counted from zero. These are guides; your background, intensity, age and exposure outside class all change the number. Weeks mean little until you know the hours per week.

**Are there free English courses in Istanbul for beginners?**
Yes, through Enstitü İstanbul İSMEK and some district municipalities, but places, terms and conditions vary, and some courses are limited by residence or age. Check current details directly with the institution before you plan around them.

**Can I join an English conversation club if I know no English?**
You can, but most clubs assume you have basic phrases. At zero, a beginners' group or a structured course will give you more speaking time and less silence.

**Is drama a replacement for a language course?**
No. Drama works on speaking, confidence and phrases in context. A course or school covers grammar, reading and writing systematically. For most beginners, the two work best together.
